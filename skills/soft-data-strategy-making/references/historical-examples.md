# Historical Examples of Hard Data Limitations

## GE's Aggregation Problem (Pre-1980)

### The Restructuring
General Electric reorganized from functional/divisional structure to Strategic Business Units (SBUs), then added Sectors above SBUs. The explicit goal: increase aggregation so top management could comprehend the portfolio.

### The Cost
As observed in the original analysis: "a great deal is lost in such aggregating, often the essence of the information itself."

**What was lost:**
- Specific competitive dynamics in individual markets
- True capabilities and constraints of underlying businesses
- Early warning signals visible at operational level but invisible at sector level
- Nuanced understanding of why numbers moved

**Management consequence:** Leaders made portfolio decisions on abstracted financial returns without understanding the operational realities driving those returns. Strategic coherence suffered.

### Lesson
Aggregation for comprehension trades understanding for convenience. Effective strategists must drill down to levels where essential information survives.

## Richard Neustadt on Presidential Information

From *Presidential Power* (1960), regarding what U.S. Presidents need for effective decision-making:

> "It is not information of general sort that helps... not summaries, not surveys, not the bland amalgams processed for popular consumption by newspapers, magazines, and television. Rather … it is the odds and ends of tangible detail that pieced together in his mind illuminate the underside of issues... He must become his own director of his own central intelligence."

**Applied to management:**
- Staff-prepared summaries serve communication, not decision quality
- The "underside of issues"—the political realities, personal motivations, unspoken concerns—require direct collection of tangible detail
- No staff function can substitute for the leader's own information network

## Eli Devons on WWII Aircraft Production Statistics

From *Planning in Practice* (1950), regarding British Ministry of Aircraft Production:

### The Data Creation Process
Despite "arbitrary assumptions made" in data collection methodology, "once a figure was put forward … it soon became accepted as the 'agreed figure,' since no one was able by rational argument to demonstrate that it was wrong... And once the figures were called 'statistics', they acquired the authority and sanctity of Holy Writ."

### The Mechanism of False Precision
1. **Arbitrary definitions:** What counts as "production"? Airframe complete? Engine installed? Accepted by inspector?
2. **Aggregation across types:** Fighters and bombers combined into "aircraft"
3. **Time period selection:** Monthly averages smoothing critical production surges or failures
4. **Definitional drift:** Standards changed to improve reported numbers without changing actual capability

### Why Rational Argument Failed
No one could prove the figures wrong because:
- The underlying assumptions were buried in process history
- Alternative figures required equally arbitrary assumptions
- Challenging data implied challenging the data collectors
- The need for "agreed figures" for coordination outweighed accuracy concerns

### Modern Parallels
- **Revenue recognition:** When does a sale count? Shipped? Billed? Paid? Contract signed?
- **Customer satisfaction:** Average scores masking distributional problems
- **Employee productivity:** Output measures missing quality, collaboration, innovation
- **Market share:** Definition games (revenue vs. unit vs. specific segments)

## The Customer Lunch Example

### Scenario
A marketing manager learns through informal network that a major customer was seen lunching with a competitor. No hard data exists—no contract signed, no order lost, no complaint registered.

### Hard Data Path
Wait for quarterly business review or attrition report. Result: competitor has six-month head start, incumbent is reactive, recovery expensive or impossible.

### Soft Data Path
Act immediately: call customer, probe for concerns, adjust terms, increase engagement. Cost: minimal. Potential benefit: retention of significant relationship.

### The Asymmetry
Soft signals of problems arrive months before hard confirmation. In competitive markets, that time advantage determines outcomes. The rational response to soft intelligence is immediate action, not waiting for statistical significance.

## The Single Disgruntled Customer

### Pattern
Market research shows 87% satisfaction, trending up. One customer sends lengthy, emotional complaint letter describing specific failures.

### Hard Data Response
Satisfaction scores acceptable; complaint is outlier; no action needed.

### Soft Data Response
Investigate complaint deeply. Often reveals:
- Systemic problem invisible to aggregate metrics (specific use case, geography, time period)
- Emerging competitor advantage
- Internal process breakdown not captured in standard measures
- Innovation opportunity from unmet need

### Research Confirmation
Fred Reichheld's work on the "recommendation question" (would you recommend us?) showed that qualitative intensity often predicts behavior better than quantitative satisfaction scores. The vocal minority frequently indicates what the silent majority will eventually do.
